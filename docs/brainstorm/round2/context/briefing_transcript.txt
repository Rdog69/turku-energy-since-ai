--- CHALLENGE PRESENTATION: POWER SYSTEM IMBALANCE PRICE FORECAST ---

Riku / Kalle:
Yeah, about the challenge: "Predict the power system imbalance, build an imbalance price forecast." Here we have... well, there's lots of slides, but this is the main thing that you need to understand to be able to build.

You may think that some of you might be familiar with spot price or day-ahead price, and basically it's the price that you are buying electricity from if you have spot-based pricing. It's not the end; it's just the beginning. Electricity has roughly five to seven different kinds of marketplaces after the day-ahead market, and that's the issue we are working with.

Basically, in Nord Pool they calculate how much the production is in Finland, how much the consumption is, and how much electricity we can transfer from other countries like Estonia or Sweden. That sets the balance situation the day before. As you may know, forecasts are always wrong, especially nowadays when we have a lot of renewable, changing power supply like wind turbines and solar panels—the weather forecast changes sometimes quite a lot.

Why is this an issue? Because our power system works such that we must always maintain a balance between production and consumption. If our forecast from the day before changes, we have to purchase more, sell our surplus, produce more, or consume more (e.g. with electric boilers). But in reality, we still have to balance consumption and production, and we don't know the real exact results until it has been measured backwards.

If we aren't able to forecast or make adjustments, then we have to buy or sell electricity at the imbalance price. If we are on the wrong side, it gets more expensive—that's the business case we are dealing with.

If there is not enough production (more consumption than production), we have up-regulation, and the price is above the spot price. So for a producer, it is good business to produce more at a higher price. Vice versa, if there is more production than consumption, the price is lower than the spot/day-ahead price (down-regulation), so it's cheaper to consume more or turn down production if your fuel costs are higher than the down-regulation price.


--- FORECASTING REQUIREMENTS & TASK SPECIFICATIONS ---

Task Overview:
- You are forecasting the imbalance price and also forecasting the uncertainty interval (10% to 90%, so an 80% gap).
- Spot exceptional prices: when the price difference between the imbalance price and day-ahead spot price is 50 €/MWh or more above or below. Big price differences present the biggest risk for forecasting errors.
- Forecast horizon: For each hour, make predictions 10 hours into the future at 15-minute resolution (40 predictions per hour).

Level 1 vs. Level 2:
- Level 1: CSV file containing number and text inputs for the predictions.
- Level 2: An interactive dashboard showing key drivers and text popups of what is happening. You can make it with selected time views or a sliding dial across the testing period.

Data & Timeline Specs:
- Testing period: October 5th to November 1st (673 hours total, accounting for daylight savings time change in Finland).
- Required Level 1 CSV output rows: 673 hours x 40 predictions (15-min intervals) = 26,920 rows.
- Training data provided: June 1st to October 1st.
- Validation period: September 7th to October 4th.
- Why training data starts June 1st: Fingrid (the Finnish transmission system operator) changed the way imbalance prices are calculated at the beginning of June. Data prior to June 1st reflects an older calculation model.

Data Freshness & Usability Rules:
- Spot prices table includes unit, value, time period, and publication timestamp. In Finland, spot prices are published around 2:00 PM local time for the next day.

--- QUESTION & ANSWER SESSION ---

Q: Why was the data before June 1st discarded?
A: Imbalance prices are calculated from reserve markets called aFRR (automatic Frequency Restoration Reserve) and mFRR (manual Frequency Restoration Reserve). Fingrid shifted the model toward a more volume-based price calculation dominated by the manual (mFRR) side, which has significantly higher volume. Prior to June, the automatic side had less volume, causing extreme price spikes/drops.

Q: Do you already have a forecasting solution in place?
A: Yes, we currently outsource imbalance price forecasting to external vendors. However, because it is outsourced, we don't have full visibility into the model's inner workings. We want our own in-house solution to understand the underlying drivers and make better operational decisions.

Q: What stopped Turku Energia from creating its own solution earlier?
A: Resource constraints. Our electricity purchasing team consists of just three people (one financial, one physical, and one overall), so our internal R&D capacity is limited.

Q: How accurate is your current outsourced solution?
A: We evaluate it retroactively when actual published prices come out. The current error margin is still quite large, which is why we are seeking a better solution through this challenge.

Q: How does Turku Energia compare in size to other Finnish energy companies like Helen?
A: We are smaller than Helen, but within our region we are the dominant energy provider. In the context of Finland overall, we are a medium-sized player.

Q: Is there a specific numeric accuracy threshold (e.g. 70% accuracy) that would make a forecast "good enough"?
A: There is no single numeric threshold. Forecasting quality is relative and evaluated by comparing model predictions against baselines and alternative solutions.

Q: Is a forecast made 1 hour before delivery useful even if accuracy is lower?
A: Yes. Predictions closer to real-time (1 hour ahead vs 10 hours ahead) are naturally more precise and very useful for operational adjustments.

Q: Is the main motivation behind this project profit or cost reduction?
A: Primarily cost reduction. Forecasting errors in imbalance prices are very costly for our purchasing operations; narrowing the error reduces unnecessary costs and improves our overall business efficiency.

Q: What is the main contributor to imbalance forecasting errors?
A: Weather—specifically rapid, unpredicted changes in wind production. In Finland, a massive portion of wind power capacity is concentrated in the Ostrobothnia (Pohjanmaa) region. A sudden shift in rain or wind speed over that concentrated area causes huge swings in power generation, directly impacting the imbalance price.

Q: When do operational decisions need to be made?
A: In actual operations, decisions are typically made 1 to 2 hours before gate closure.
