# Jury Q&A preparation

> Drafted by a Featherless worker (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash) from our docs; checked by the coordinator. Numbers are validation-period (7 Sep – 4 Oct) for `v6_hf`, the pipeline model, unless a row names another model. TODO(team): v6_hf or team5050 — which file is submitted.

Nineteen hard questions a mixed energy-market / ML jury would ask in a 3-minute Q&A, with answers grounded in the attached documents. Confidence reflects how well our own evidence supports the answer, not how much we like it.

---

### 1. "You model the gap to spot rather than the price. Why is that legitimate, and what would break it?"

**Answer:** The imbalance price is the day-ahead spot price plus a jump: up-regulation prices above spot, down-regulation below, and when neither direction dominates the price follows spot; since 1 June 2026, when both mFRR and aFRR dominate, it is the volume-weighted average of their energy prices. Spot is known for all 40 steps at every hourly origin (we checked every origin 2 Jun – 1 Nov), and a constant gap on top of spot beats the persistence baseline by a third (MAE 44.29 vs 66.20). So we forecast only what the day-ahead plan missed in Finland, which is where the new information actually is. What would break it is a change to the settlement rule itself, or the "no dominating direction" case becoming common rather than the ~0.2 % we assume. **[confidence: high]**

### 2. "Your model only improves MAE from 44.29 to 40.67 over a constant gap on spot. Is the machine learning doing anything?"

**Answer:** Honest answer: it adds about 3.6 €/MWh of MAE (44.29 → 40.67), and the gain came in small steps, not from one big idea: LightGBM 43.23, calibrated 42.89, smaller and slower trees 41.82, learned wind production 41.08, 15 months of pre-June history 40.67 (`docs/RESULTS.md`). The model earns its place most on the other three metrics: pinball 14.99 → 11.19, spike Brier 0.1132 → 0.0856, direction Brier 0.4508 → 0.3892. Our own conclusion is that better MAE needs new information (history, fresher system state, better weather), not a fancier model. **[confidence: high]**

### 3. "Why gradient boosting and not a deep or foundation time-series model?"

**Answer:** We have about four months of data (roughly 12,000 training quarters for the spike analysis), one target series, and a strong deterministic anchor already explaining two-thirds of the error — that is a small-data, tabular problem, and LightGBM gives us quantiles plus calibrated probabilities and TreeSHAP drivers in one stack. 50 of the 80 automatic points (pinball 20, spike Brier 20, direction Brier 10) reward calibrated uncertainty, so variance reduction and honest uncertainty matter more than model capacity. A Chronos/TimesFM challenger was planned but not run, so we can't claim it wouldn't have helped. **[confidence: medium — the data-size and metric arguments are in our docs; the explicit "why not DL" reasoning is our reconstruction, not a measured comparison]**

### 4. "How do you actually guarantee you never used data before it was published?"

**Answer:** Every feature is read through one access layer (`SeriesIndex.value_at` / `latest` in `src/data/availability.py`), which returns a value only if its `first_usable_origin_utc ≤ t`; no feature uses a fixed shift. The key test is a truncation test: for sampled origins (data start, spot publication hours, the DST change, period boundaries, plus random hours) we rebuild features from the full data and again from data with every row published after `t` deleted — the two must be identical, and the weather features are covered too. A second test compares the fast lookups against the organisers' slow `rows_available_at` logic, and further tests check `data_cutoff_utc` is before the origin, that targets are never features, and that training rows were published before the training cut-off. **[confidence: high]**

### 5. "Your external series use *your own* publication delays — Fingrid real-time at 30 minutes, ECMWF at 8 hours. What if the organisers reject those?"

**Answer:** Those delays are assumptions, and we say so: 30 min is copied from the organisers' own delay for measured Fingrid data (Fingrid actually publishes every 1–3 min), and our measured ECMWF init-to-availability was about 7.1 h against our 8 h rule — both rounded up to the next full hour and conservative. We don't know yet whether the organisers accept externally sourced series with our documented delay, because we have not had that answer; what would settle it is an organisers' ruling. The fallback is already measured: dropping Fingrid real-time entirely costs us 0.55 €/MWh (`split_all` 42.53 against the package-plus-weather model 43.08) and 0.0077 direction Brier. This is the ablation of our `split_all` family; `v6_hf` also uses the real-time data in its short model. **[confidence: high on the numbers, and the uncertainty is stated as uncertainty]**

### 6. "50 points ride on calibrated uncertainty. How do you get coverage to 0.80, and is that number honest?"

**Answer:** The raw LightGBM quantiles were far too narrow (coverage 0.527), so we hold out the last 14 days of the training window, fit on the earlier data, and learn Platt scaling for the three probabilities plus P10/P50/P90 offsets in three step bands (1–8, 9–20, 21–40); then we refit on the full window and apply those maps. In our package-only chain that took coverage from 0.527 to about 0.80 and the spike Brier from 0.1063 to 0.0939. `v6_hf` has coverage 0.771 on validation (0.770 in August), a little under the 80 % target, while its scored pinball loss is 5.8 % better than `v4_n50`'s; `team5050` has 0.801. The calibration window is the last 28 days, and the spike correction is shrunk when the window holds few spike quarters. The honest caveat is that the maps are learned on data before the test period, so the validation window is our only genuinely held-out evidence — see the drift question next. **[confidence: high on the mechanism, medium on generalisation]**

### 7. "You picked the best of about 37 variants on a single four-week window, and your calibration offsets roughly doubled into October. Isn't this overfitting?"

**Answer:** Yes, that is the main threat to our numbers and we flag it in the report rather than hiding it: choosing the best of about 37 variants on 7 Sep – 4 Oct makes the result optimistic. Since round 3 every change also had to win on August. Concretely, the 14-day holdout before the test (20 Sep – 4 Oct) was volatile, so the learned P90 offset is +28 to +36 €/MWh there versus +14 to +21 for validation — either correct adaptation to the autumn regime or overfitting to two noisy weeks. We don't know yet which; the August fold (where `v6_hf` scores 27.74 MAE), and a week-by-week breakdown of the test period would answer it. **[confidence: high — this is our own stated limitation]**

### 8. "How do you model spikes, and what about spikes that don't match the dominant direction?"

**Answer:** We model them as `P(spike high) = P(up) × P(gap > 50 | up)` and symmetrically for low, which keeps them consistent with the direction forecast and sum to at most 1; the product form beat direct spike classifiers (Brier 0.0945 vs 0.0971). In about 12,000 training quarters only 7 high spikes fall outside up-regulation and 18 low spikes outside down-regulation, so tying spikes to direction is empirically the right structure. The risk we can't dismiss is that validation spike shares (12.1 % / 13.0 %) are well above training (9.0 % / 7.3 %), so this is a regime the models have seen little of. **[confidence: high on the model form, medium on the tail counts]**

### 9. "Your direction Brier only improves 14 % over the baseline, and `p_none` is a constant. Are those probabilities useful?"

**Answer:** `p_none` is a constant at the training share of about 0.2 % because "no dominating direction" almost never happens, and the remaining mass is split by a binary up-classifier. Direction is our weakest metric — 0.3892 for `v6_hf` vs the baseline's 0.4509 (`team5050` 0.3803) — and we'd rather say that than dress it up; on the first two hours a separate short-horizon model does much better (hour 1: 0.342 vs 0.408 in the `split` ablation). Minute-level imbalance data (Fingrid 198/186, BRP plan error) gave only a small gain in our `split_all_rt_icon` (direction 0.4011 → 0.3978) and is not in `v6_hf`. **[confidence: high]**

### 10. "The clocks change on 25 October, inside your test period. How do you handle that?"

**Answer:** The test period has 673 origins, exactly one more than 28 × 24, because of the 25-hour local day, and our features read the package's local calendar (`cal_hour_local`, `cal_weekday_local`, `cal_is_dst`), which the organisers computed from Finnish local time, so the hour-of-day signal should stay correct. Our leakage test deliberately includes origins around the DST change. We have not checked forecast quality on that day separately, and we honestly don't know yet whether 25 Oct is a weak spot — a per-day error breakdown over the test period would answer it. **[confidence: high on the mechanics, low on the quality claim]**

### 11. "Which of your ideas failed, and which sources did you reject?"

**Answer:** Recency weights (τ = 30 days) improved MAE but hurt pinball, coverage and both probability scores, so we dropped them; giving Fingrid real-time features to *every* step made things worse (MAE 43.43) because the signal is stale by hour 3, which is why we split the horizon at step 8. We never used Fingrid's continuously updated forecasts (245, 241) because only the latest version is stored and it would leak, and we avoided the Open-Meteo Historical Forecast API for the same reason. We also rejected the free spot-price mirror (licence says external use is prohibited, and pre-June spot would be needed to compute 2025 gaps) — separately, the organisers allowed pre-June data on 7 Nov (`QUESTIONS.md` Q1), so `v6_hf` now trains on it; the older imbalance prices are recomputed under the June 2026 rule (99.5 % match). **[confidence: high]**

### 12. "What does the dashboard actually give a shift supervisor that a price chart doesn't?"

**Answer:** Level 2 is 20 % of the score and is judged on clarity for a shift supervisor or trading specialist, two time spans (now / next 10 h and past performance over a day, week and month), time travel back into the past, and inventiveness. Ours adds spike risk per hour, rule-based alerts when the balancing need or forecast changes materially, plain-language briefings, and review suggestions such as "risk of an exceptionally high price has increased for 16:00–18:00, driven by a lower wind forecast, so check the production plan". Because we have no plant or position data, these are explicitly suggestions to **review**, never instructions. Our documents record no user test with a real operator, so we don't yet know how a shift supervisor would actually use it — that would need a Turku Energia operator to try it. **[confidence: high on scope, low on measured usefulness]**

### 13. "Where is the LLM used, and what stops it hallucinating a price?"

**Answer:** It writes a 2–3 sentence briefing per forecast hour on the dashboard (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct via Featherless) and nothing else — it never sees raw data, no spot prices and no absolute price forecasts, only a deterministic fact sheet of expected *gaps* to spot, probabilities, uncertainty width, rule-based alerts and feature labels. It cannot change any forecast value: the scored CSV is written by the backtest before the LLM runs, and `top_drivers` comes from LightGBM's TreeSHAP, not from the model. Every number in its output is checked against the fact sheet and degenerate text is rejected, falling back to a template briefing. **[confidence: high]**

### 14. "The whole forecast is anchored on Nord Pool spot, which is licensed. Can you ship this?"

**Answer:** Spot is licensed to Turku Energia for hackathon use only, so the repo stays private, `results/` (which contains spot) is git-ignored, no document or log quotes spot values, and the hosted Lovable preview uses demo data with a synthetic spot curve. Anything leaving the machine — LLM prompts, n8n alert payloads — is spot-free by code guard, and we send only gaps and probabilities. One thing we don't know yet: whether forecasts *derived* from spot may leave the machine at all — we have asked no one, and an organisers' clarification would settle it. Everyone deletes their copy after the event. **[confidence: medium — the controls are in the repo, but the derived-data question is open]**

### 15. "With more time, or with Turku Energia's internal data, what would you do?"

**Answer:** Technically: Nord Pool UMM outage messages, which are fetched but used in only about 20 training quarters today, and real-time Fingrid and weather data for the pre-June history rows, which are empty there. The 15 months of pre-June history are already in `v6_hf` and help the band most. Pooled calibration was tested and rejected; the real-time imbalance series (Fingrid 198/186) and plan error (242) gave a small gain in `split_all_rt_icon`. With Turku Energia's own capacity, marginal costs, contracts and trading position, we could move the dashboard from "suggestions to review" to capacity-aware recommendations — that is the single biggest step we can't take from public data. **[confidence: high]**

### 16. "You train on 15 months of history with a recomputed imbalance price. How do you know that doesn't leak or mislead?"

**Answer:** Leak: the history goes through the same availability rule as everything else. `load_history` gives every measured history row a first-usable time no earlier than the package's own rule for that minute of the hour, and a truncation leak test covers history origins (`tests/test_availability.py`). Misleading: the pricing rule changed on 1 June 2026, so the older imbalance prices are recomputed with the new volume-weighted rule. That target matches the official price on 99.5 % of Jun–Oct 2026 quarters; we cannot check the match before June. A `regime_2026` flag (1 for deliveries from 1 Jun 2026) tells the model which rule a row was priced under, and `p_none` is still taken from June onwards only. Pre-June rows have no ENTSO-E neighbour series, weather runs or Fingrid real-time data, so those features are empty there. The check that it helps: the history improved both months, September (+2.26 % weighted by points) and August (+2.96 %), and most on the band (pinball −6 %). The organisers allowed pre-June data on 7 Nov (`QUESTIONS.md` Q1). **[confidence: high on the leak controls, medium on the recomputed target before June]**

### 17. "What is `MedianFrom`, and isn't taking the median from a different model cherry-picking?"

**Answer:** `MedianFrom` takes the P50 and its drivers from a twin model that also sees wind in Sweden, Norway, Denmark and Estonia. The band and the probabilities come from the model without that wind. It is not a pick of the best-looking output after the fact: the rule was decided from two separate months. Nordic wind improved the median in both September and August and made the band worse in both, so we use it where it helps and not where it hurts. In August the median was better in 4 of 5 weeks; the band was worse overall, mainly because of one bad day (31 Aug). The gain is small (+0.09 % weighted in September, +0.45 % in August). We still chose it among about 37 variants on the validation month, so the same optimism caveat as question 7 applies. **[confidence: high on the numbers, medium on how large the gain will be on the test month]**

### 18. "Your August numbers for `team5050` come from a lab scorer. Are the two months comparable?"

**Answer:** Partly. The scoreboard (`scoreboard/README.md`) sets one set of rules for both months: the same data cut-offs, the same weights (MAE 30, pinball 20, spikes 20, direction 10) and the same baseline method. Official scores come from the organisers' `score.py`; a lab score is allowed only when someone cannot run it, and when a model has both, the official one counts. `team5050` has a September entry scored officially (40.97 / 11.63 / 0.0861 / 0.3803) and an August entry from Abdo's lab scorer (27.27 / 6.57 / 0.0677 / 0.4281, no coverage column). His August forecast file is not in git, so we could not rescore it. The only check we have is that his lab scorer reproduces our official August scores for `v4_n50` exactly. So the scoreboard's lead for `team5050` (31.1 % average against 30.9 % for `v6_hf`) mixes one official and one lab month, and the 0.2 point gap is too small to call a winner from. **[confidence: high on the facts, low on who is really ahead]**

### 19. "What did the live-imbalance features (Fingrid 198/186) add?"

**Answer:** A little, and only close to the origin. In `split_all_rt` (live surplus/deficit, production against the BRP plan, hydro change; short model only) fold A moved from 42.53 to 42.52 MAE and direction Brier from 0.4011 to 0.4005, and fold B improved spikes (0.0750 to 0.0732) and direction (0.4491 to 0.4480) with MAE and pinball unchanged. The gain sits in hours 1–2 (hour 1 direction 0.3471 to 0.3431). It was consistent in sign across metrics and both folds but small. Together with ICON-EU (`split_all_rt_icon`) it gave MAE 42.14 and direction 0.3978 on fold A. The surplus/deficit series alone has almost no rank correlation with the next hour's gap (|rho| below 0.05); hydro change is the clearer signal. These features are not used in `v6_hf`; Frank's models took only our ICON-EU features. **[confidence: high]**

## Three things to say in every answer

1. **We beat the persistence baseline on all four automatic metrics**, and we quote the numbers unprompted: MAE 66.20 → 40.67, pinball 24.18 → 11.19, spike Brier 0.1132 → 0.0856, direction Brier 0.4509 → 0.3892 for `v6_hf` (validation, 7 Sep – 4 Oct; 35.7 % better weighted by points in September, 26.0 % in August).
2. **Nothing touches data before it was published**, enforced in one access layer and verified by a truncation test that rebuilds every feature with future rows deleted — a leak means an automatic zero, which is why we test it rather than promise it.
3. **We separate measured from assumed.** When something is an assumption (external publication delays, calibration generalising to October, the dashboard's value in a real control room) we say "we don't know yet" and name what would settle it — that is stronger than a confident guess in front of energy-market experts.
